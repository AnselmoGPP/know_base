## Introduction

### Pragmatism

**Pragmatism** claims that an ideology or proposition is true if it works satisfactorily. The meaning of a proposition is found in the practical consequences of accepting it, and unpractical ideas have to be rejected. It opposes to empiricism

Main **authors** are:

- **Classical pragmatism**:
  - Charles Sanders Peirce (1839-1914)
  - William James (1842-1910)
  - John Dewey (1859-1952)
- **Post-Deweyan pragmatism**:
  - W.V.O. Quine (1908-2000)
  - Richard Rorty (1931-2007).

Pragmatists don't have a unified thought. They take different views on major issues (truth, tealism, skepticism, perception, justification, fallibilism, realism, conceptual schemes, function of philosophy…). There is no pragmatist creed, no neat list of articles or essential tenets endorsed by all pragmatists and only pragmatists. Detractors consider that this diversity shows that pragmatism stands for little in particular.

**Themes and theses**: Some ideas have loomed large in the pragmatist tradition, though they're not exclusive property of pragmatists, nor they are endorsed by all pragmatists. Some themes and theses to which many pragmatists have attached are the following:

- **A Method and A Maxim**: 

**Pragmatic method**: What concrete practical difference would it make if my theory were true and its rivals false? If there's no difference, there is no genuine (that is, non-verbal) disagreement, and hence no genuine problem.

**Pragmatic maxim**: It points to a broadly verificationist conception of linguistic meaning. No sense can be made of the idea that there're facts which are unknowable in principle (truths no one can ever confirm and could have no bearing on our conduct or experience). Talking about inaccessible Kantian things-in-themselves, a true world (Nietzsche) forever hidden behind the veil of phenomena, is useless or idle.

Theories and models are to be judged primarily by their fruits and consequences. Scientific theories are instruments or tools for coping with reality (James and Dewey). The utility of a theory is a matter of its problem-solving power, not the emotional consolation or subjective comfort it delivers (Dewey). A variant says that theories never positively justified may still be rationally accepted if repeated attempts to falsify them failed (Popper).

- **Anti-Cartesianism**:

**Mind**: According to Cartesianism, the mind is a self-contained sphere whose contents ("ideas" or "impressions") are irredeemably subjective and private, utterly sundered form the public and objective world they purport to represent. Considering the mind a world unto itself makes a host of problems the empiricists have long struggled with (solipsism, skepticism, realism, idealism…). Pragmatists oppose to this picture. They want to free philosophy from optional assumptions that generate insoluble and unreal problems.

**Truth**: Pragmatists find the Cartesian "quest for certainty" (Dewey) quixotic. All beliefs and theories are best treated as working hypotheses which may need to be modified—refined, revised, or rejected—in light of future inquiry and experience. Some arguments they provide to defend such fallibilism are:

  - Even our best theories (Euclidean geometry, Newtonian physics…) have needed significant and unexpected revisions.
  - If scientific theories are dramatically underdetermined by data, we cannot know if we have chosen the right theory.
  - Truth is what would be accepted at the end of inquiry, but we cannot really know that we reached the end of inquiry.
  - Ascriptions of certainty block the road of inquiry, keeping us from making progress, if it's still possible.
  - Fallibilism is the only sane alternative to cocksure dogmatism, and the fanaticism, intolerance, and violence to which it can lead.

**Global doubt**: Pragmatists oppose the Cartesian idea that philosophy should begin with bold global doubt. Inquiry can start only when there's some actual or living doubt, but we cannot doubt everything at once (though nothing cannot be doubt during our inquiries). Inquirers are mariners who must repair their raft plank by plank on the open sea, without disembarking and analysing from an external standpoint (Otto Neurath). We begin in the middle of things. Our starting points are contingent and historically conditioned inheritances. Philosophy is not prior to science, but continuous with it. There's no pure reason or intuition, no reality innaccessible to science for philosophy to study. Philosophers don't legislate, they make explicit the norms and methods implicit inour best current practice.

**Global skepticism**: Cartesians think that we cannot know anything about the external world because we can never know that we're not dreaming. Pragmatists have different considerations about this skepticism: it's a proof of the futile quest for certainty (Dewey, Rescher), it rests on an untenable Cartesian philosophy of mind (Rorty, Davidson), it presupposes of a discredited correspondence theory of truth (Rorty), believing in an external world is justified only if it "works" or best explains our sensory experience (James, Schiller, Quine), the problem of the external world cannot be formulated unless it's already assumed that it exists (Dewey), thinking about truths no one could ever know is useless (Peirce), and massive error about the world is inconceivable (Putnam, Davidson).

- **The Kantian inheritance**:

**Framework**: Pragmatists think that Kant was right to say that the world must be interpreted with the aid of a scheme of basic categories, but consider that he was wrong to suggest that this framework is sacrosanct, immutable, or necessary. Our categories and theories are our creations, not simply read off from the world, and they reflect purposes and interest in a given context. Frameworks can change and be replaced. There is more than one way to conceptualize the world and its content. The world doesn't impose some unique description on us; rather, we choose how the world is described. Objects exist relative to conceptual shemes or frameworks (Putnam).

**Raw experience**: Empiricism consider that our beliefs about the world ultimately derive their justification from perception. I believe that the cat is on the mat not because of another belief or judgment, but because I saw it (visual experience). Since experience is given from without, it can justify one's basic beliefs (beliefs whose justification doesn't derive from other beliefs). Pragmatists oppose to this because they consider that perceive is really to interprete and hence to classify. Thus, we cannot verify theories or worldviews by comparing them with some raw, unsullied sensuous "Given". Hence, experience cannot serve as a basic, belief-independent source of justification. There're no foundational beliefs (beliefs that don't depend on other beliefs for their justification) that justify or support all other beliefs. Objections: non-inferential knowledge is a fiction, knowledge is more like a coherent web than a hierarchically structure building, there're no certain foundations for knowledge (since fallibilism is true), foundational beliefs cannot be justified by appealing to perceptual experience (since the "Given" is a myth), and knowledge has no overall or non-contextual structure. 

- **The Spectator theory of knowledge**:

**Spectator theory of knowledge**: Knowing is akin to seeing or beholding. The knower aims to reflect or duplicate the world without altering it, he survey or contemplate things from a practically disengaged and disinterested standpoint. Observation is reception. The mind is fundamentally passive in perception.

Pragmatists oppose to this theory. Knowledge is the product of inquiry, a problem-solving process by means of which we move from doubt to belief. Inquiry cannot proceed effectively unless we experiment (manipulate or change reality). Knowers must be agents. Dewey proposes the alternative "Experimental theory of knowledge". Observe is to select (looking for something). Observation requires action. Observers are bound to discriminate, guided by interest, expectation, and theory. Perceivers are explorers, not mirrors. Acceptance of a theory always precedes and directs observation, so theories are not derived from independently discovered data or facts.

Facts are stubborn things with powerful authority. Observed facts are impressed on our mind. Facts are the antidote to prejudice and the cure for bias. The justificatory status of beliefs is partly a function of how well they cohere or fit with entrenched beliefs or theory. However, no "fact" can be admitted into our mind unless it can be coherently assimilated or harmonized with beliefs we already hold (James and Schiller), which rejects the idea that the mind is a purely receptive blank slate (Locke).

- **The Correspondence theory of truth**:

**Truth** is a matter of correspondence or agreement with reality or facts (Plato).

Pragmatists consider this view vague and beset with problems. Some pragmatists consider that this theory must be abandoned, others consider it's just incomplete. Some problems of the theory:
  
  - "Correspondence" is not well understood of explicated (it's not copying, surely).
  - It makes verification and inquiry a mistery. If the "Given" is a myth, we cannot justify theories by comparing them with an unconceptualized reality.
  - It suggests that the mind forms a subjective inner representations of an objective outer order.
  - There's no extra-linguistic reality for us to represent, no mind-independent world to which our beliefs are answerable.

Some pragmatist views about truth are:

  - Truth is what "works". True hypothesis are useful (James, Dewey).
  - True opinions are those which inquirers will accept at the end of inquiry, those which we could not improve (Peirce).
  - Truth has no nature or essence. Calling a belief or theory "true" doesn't ascribe any property to it (Rorty).

### Positivism

**Positivism** was founded by August Comte (1789-1857).

A perfect society must have harmony and unity of all its members, and great variety. The more harmony there is, the more variety there must be. This is similar to a symphony and its instruments. However, what we see is chaos of conflicting elements (individual men and classes of men): communities are battlegrounds, conflicting interests and experiences, benefits at the expense of others, popular agitators, etc. Hence the problem of determining the right legislation and social action is continually being made to balance interests against one another and do justice to both. Conflicts are generally caused by interests or rights of individuals or classes. This is the essence of every practical problem. The theory of Society regards every practical problem as one of compromise between conflicting interests or rights.

**Positivistm** considers that, despite the conflict and strife, all individuals share the same essential being, destiny, origin, and idea of goodness. Since all men are "member of one body", the conflict is not really between interests, but between different elements or aspects of our common good. Our common good must include the following objectives, though modern civilization make them stand in opposition to one another:

- Growth of intelligence and realization of scientific knowledge
- Growth of capacity to appreciate and create beauty
- Growth of capacity to perform practical social duties

Comte considers social union a concrete spiritual life penetrating its members (so intellectual life by itself is an unsocial and selfish existence). However, H.S. Mellone disagrees. Mans life is divided between the egoistic and altruistic "innate" impulses, which act independently of each other and of intelligence. The only use of intelligence is to satisfy one or the other. However, our social side nature doesn't only lie in feeling. Originally, selfish feelings were stronger, but the influence of circumstances and social experiences partially suppressed them and allowed unselfish feelings grow. In an ideal future, the latter are supreme and the former annihilated.

Often, on important problems, two extreme conclusions are formed and maintained in opposition to one another (specially in politics, ethics, and philosophy). Aristotle says that, in such case, rational discrimination enables finding a middle way.
