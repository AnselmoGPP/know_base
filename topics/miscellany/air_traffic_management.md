## ANS (Air Navigation Services)

- **CNS** (Communication, Navigation, Surveillance): Used by pilots and ATCs.
  - **COM** (Air-Ground Communications System)
  - **NAV** (Navigation System)
  - **SUR** (Surveillance System)
- **ATM** (Air Traffic Management)
  - **ASM** (Air Space Management): In contact with airlines.
  - **ATFM** (Air Traffic Flow Management): In contact with airports.
  - **ATS** (Air Traffic Services): In contact with air crew.
    - **ATC services** (Air Traffic Control Services)
    - **FIS** (Flight Information Services)
    - **ADV** (Advisory service)
    - **ALRS** (Alerting Service)
- **MET** (Meteorological Servicie)
- **AIS** (Aeronautical Information Service)
- **SAR** (Search & Rescue Service)

**Glossary:**

- **ATFM**: Typically carried in 3 phases: strategic planning (months in advance: flights scheduling, fligh paths, identify most problematic hours…), pre-tactical planning (few days in advance: fine tunning the plan, decide what tactical measures will be used on the day of operation…), and tactical operations (during the day of operation: traffic actions, apply delays or restrictions…).

  - **ATFCM** (Air Traffic Flow & Capacity Management) aims to reduce overloads and maximize the use of airspace. Objectives: protect air traffic control from over-delivery and optimize available capacity. This is done by finding a balance between **capacity** (capacity: number of flights that can be handled safely and efficiently in a sector in a time period, usually an hour) and **demand** (number of flights that intend to flight in a sector during a time period). If demand exceeds capacity, one of them has to be adjusted. Usually, capacity is adjusted first; but if this is not possible, demand is reduced (delay airborne, reroute to other sectors…).

**ATC services**: To manage/control flights by ensuring a separation between each other and to the ground, and enable them to land/departure in order. Provided by ATCs (Air Traffic Controllers) in controlled airspace.

**FIS**: Up-to-date information about any conditions that may affect the flight (weather, turbulences in the area, water in the runway, ash cloud from volcano, toxic chemicals in the air, radioactivity, etc.). Provided by ATCs in any airspace (controlled or not). It’s called **AFIS** (Aerodrome FIS) if it’s provided by aerodromes with no ATC service.

**ADV**: Additional help provided in busy airspaces where more complicated conflicts happen. It includes advice to the pilots on what actions they could take to avoid collisions. This is a temporary measure until an airspace can be redesignated and control ATC service provided.

**ALRS**: When an aircraft goes missing (maybe due to an accident), certain organizations may be called for SAR (search & rescue). Three levels of alert:

- **Incefa** (Uncertainty phase): The aircraft didn’t arrive at the expected time to the aerodrome or didn’t send an expected report at a given point. Communication cannot be stablished. Information gathered: last known position and if it communicated with another unit. This enquiries often result in the aircraft being found safely on the ground or in contact with another ATC unit.
- **Alerfa**: If the aircraft is not found, too much time passed, or some information indicates the aircraft experiences difficulties, the SAR units are alerted and they get prepared themselves.
- **Detresfa**: If more time passes, or some information indicates a too serious situation, a full SAR operation is activated.

**SAR**: Responsability of the State. Organizations and units that search for an aircraft or conduct rescue operations (other public or private resources may be used). All missions are coordinated from the Rescue Coordination Centre. Some services provided are: monitoring distress situations, communicating/coordinating SAR operations, initial medical assistance/evacuation, etc.

**AIS**: Airlines and pilots need some information for planning and carrying the flight safely. Each State publish this information in their AIS in a standardized format (defined by ICAO). It contains:

- Layout of aerodromes
- Airspaces and their classifications
- Radiofrequencies
- Rules for the country
- Procedures for airspaces and aerodromes
- Types of on-board equipment allowed
- **NOTAM** (Notices To Airmen): Temperature changes, taxi ways closed, launch of weather balloons, avoidance of areas for an air display, navigation aids not available, etc.
- etc.

**MET**: Most significant factors affecting flight are: wind, air temperature, visibility, and hazards. The metheorological office provides wheather information to pilots and controllers. Also, metheorological services around the world share information with each other, so that the local office can provide briefings to pilota covering all the flight route. Information provided: metheorological forecasts, briefs, observations, reports on significant weather phenomena. This includes:

- Volcano activity (pre-eruption, eruption, ash clouds, …).
- METAR: Weather conditions at an aerodrome, produced each hour.
- TAR: Forecast the changes over 24 hours, produced every 6 hour.
- SIGMENT: Significant and potentially hazardous weather (thunder storms, severe icing, sandstorms, severe turbulence, heavy hail, …).

## Airspace

Not all flights need control. Space can be:

- **Controlled**: ATCs provide extensive information (FIS) and keep flights separated from each other (ATC service).
- **Non-controlled**: Flights fly wherever they want, but they’re responsible of keeping them separated from any other flight. ATCs provide some information (FIS): weather and close traffic.

**Controlled airspace** chunks:

- **Control zone**: Around airports. From ground level to up to 4000 ft (or even 10000 ft).
- **Terminal control area**: Above one or more aerodromes.
- **Airways**: Corridors of airspace between aerodromes. About 10 nm width.
- **Control area**: Collection of Terminal control areas and Airways (some ATCs consider it to be just the area above a collection of Terminal control areas).
- **Flight information region (FIR)**: Area containing the area above one Terminal control area and its surroundings. All the FIRs together cover the entire airspace. It is divided into 2 sections depending upon the altitude: Upper information region (UIR) and FIR.

**ATS routes** (Air Traffic Space routes): They join together different locations, similar to highways in the sky, that fights traverse.

**AIP** (Aeronautical Information Publication): Publishes the structure of the airspace and ATS routes.

**Ways of flying**:

- **VFR** (Visual Flight Rules): To fly using maps and identifying land-marks through the window.
- **IFR** (Instruments Flight Rules): To fly using primarily navigation instruments on board.

ATCs provide a specific **kind of control** in a given airspace chunk controlled by them:

- **A**: Only IFR flights allowed and separated
- **B**: Both IFR and VFR flights allowed and separated
- **E**: Both IFR and VFR flights allowed. IFRs are separated between each other, but VFR have to keep themselves separated from everybode. IFRs are informed of close VFRs.

**FUA** (Flexible Use of Airspace): Both civilian and military have to share the same airspace. FUA is based in user’s requirements and adaptable space structures and procedures, suited for temporary allocation and utilization. It reinforces **ASM** (Airspace Management) on different levels (strategic, pre-tactical, tactical) with creation of a pan-european plan. It facilitates coordination between both types of users, avoiding segregation as much as feasible. It relies on national **AMCs** (Airspace Management Cells) for daily allocation of flexible airspace structures. In the future, AFUA (Advanced FUA) will further enhance bot FUA operations and network performance. AMCs comprise:

- Civil service providers: Air traffic management.
- Civil airspace users: Civil flight planning.
- Military users: Military flight planning.
- Network manager: Air traffic flow, Capacity management, Flight plan management.

## Systems, Standards, Rules

**Systems**: Pilots and ATCs need some systems (COM, NAV, SUR).

**Standards**:

- A flight is considered separated from another if it’s at least 5 nm away or 1000 ft above/below.
- In oceanic regions (or areas with little surveillance) it can be 60 nm.
- Minimum altitude allowed above areas with buildings.
- etc.

**Rules**:

- Types of clearances and instructions ATCs can give to pilots
- Kind of routes an aircraft can fly in/out of airports
- Way pilots and ATCs communicate to each other
- etc.

**ICAO** (International Civil Aviation Organization): UN organization. It stablishes the basic principles (freedoms) that allows scheduled international air services to operate across international boundaries. It stablishes standards, procedures, and recommended practices for global air navigation (it publishes the basic rules and standards for pilots and ATCs). It also supports states on developing their aviation infrastructure, produces global plans for aviation development, and monitors the air transport sector performance. ICAO is based in Montreal (USA). The world is divided in 7 ICAO regions. Some of its publications are:

- **SARPS** (Standards and Recommended Practices): Set of 19 publications (annexes) that pass a consultation process prior to being published (~2 years process). If a State doesn’t adhere to a particular standard, it has to inform ICAO and publish it in its own national **AIP** (Aeronautical Information Publication).
- **PANS** (Procedures for Air Navigation Service): Set of documents that provide guidance on how to implement the Standards (each one is associated to a specific annex). They pass a consultation process prior to being published. The most relevant PANS document is the one about ATM, which includes topics such as: aircrafts separation, surveillance systems, aerodrome control, communications, information coordination with other ATS units, emergency situations, etc.
- **Manuals** and **circulars**:  Guidance material that adds more detail to SARPS and PANS (ATCs training, aerodrome design, environment, ATM security, etc.).

Usually, the civil aviation authority of each country transcribes the SARPS and PANS into law.

**FPL** (Flight plan): ATCs need to know flight’s intentions to ensure safe and efficient services. FPLs are documents (in a standard format define by ICAO) submitted to traffic control by pilots or flight dispatchers usually days/months before the flight takes place. They have to be filled for flights that operate under IFL, in controlled space, or if it crosses international borders (it’s optional for the rest of flights), or. Some information provided by the FPL is:

- Aircraft id, aerodromes (origin, destination, alternatives), route, navigation equipment on board, flight rules, type of flight, type of aircraft, departure time, flight time length, cruising speed, cruising height level, etc.
- Supplementary information: pilo, fuel endurance, persons on board, emergency radio equipment, survival equipment, type of life jackets, survival dinghies on board, aircraft color and marking, etc.

## Controllers (ATCs)

**ATCs activities**:

- To know the intentions of the flight they control (origin, destination, route, date, time, type of aircraft, crew…). Provided by the pilot or airline operators via a flight plan.
- To monitor progress of flights in real time via radar (or other equipment) or pilot communication.
- In order to know this information, ATCs work out whether flights are or will be in conflict with other flights.
- To issue clearances and instructions to pilots to ensure no collisions and safe flights while respecting the standards and rules.
- To coordinate with other ATCs to make sure that all flights proceed safely from one airspace to the next.

**Controller types**:

- **Tower controllers**: Provide control in the vicinity of a aerodrome, ensuring safe and efficient traffic in/out of the aerodrome, separation between aircrafts, separation between aircrafts, and ground vehicles or aerodrome obstacles. Tower controllers can provide different types of aerodrome control:
  - **Clearance-delivery**: Issues departure clearences
  - **Ground movement control**: Manages aircraft and vehicles on the ground (except those on the runway or apron). It doesn’t control vehicles delivering baggage, catering, passengers, or crew to the aircraft.
  - **Air control**: For anything happening on the runways and the air around the aerodrome. Also, it issues clearances for landing, take-off, or crossing runways.
    - **Wake turbulences**: Planes create vortices in the wing tips that can affect planes nearby. Tower controllers prevent this by waiting (2-3 min) the departed aircraft turbulences to dissipate before a smaller aircraft departures.
- **Approach controllers**: Provide control to departing aircrafts climbing to join an airway, or those leaving an airway to descend to land. They typically work on Terminal areas or part of the Control zone. Two types of approach controllers: surveillance and procedural.
- **Area controllers**: Provide control to aircrafts flying between airports, in the Control area, after leaving the Terminal control area.Two types of area controllers: surveillance (uses radar or any surveillance equipment) and procedural (doesn’t use surveillance equipment). They can predict (10-20 min) conflicts between aircrafts and prevent them (change speed, change level, rerouting, vectoring).
  - **Oceanic controllers**: Provide control over large oceanic areas (limited comms, minimal surveillance, no ground-based navigation aid).

## Communications

Communications between pilots and ATCs can be **automated** (Data link system) or **spoken**.

- Information is typically communicated directly over radio.
- **ATIS** (Automatic Terminal Information Service): Some information that is repeated often can be kept broadcasting on a loop. Pilots know what frequency tune into to get this information.
- Clear communications are necessary. Letters are communicated using a **phonetic alphabet**.
- Each flying aircraft must have a **callsign** (name for ATC) (G-LKOE). Once ATC contact is made, it’s shortened (G-OE). Private aircrafts use their registration name as callsign, airlines use their airline name + flight number (DLH4165 for ATCs, LH4165 for passengers) (ATCs decode it when speaking: DLH4165 == Lufthansa4165)
- **Numbers** are used for flight levels, altitudes, headings, etc. Numbers are usually trasmitted digit by digit (flight levels, wind speed and direction, temperature, pressure…) except for visibility and cloudbase.
- ATCs manage aircrafts issuing **clearances** and **instructions**. Clearances (taxi, route, take-off, climb…) are transmitted with standard words & phrases.

## Keeping separation

**Labels** offer a lot of information and they can be configured in many different ways depending on what the controller needs. A simple label (v. g.: callsign + level + speed) is usually used for just monitoring the flight’s progress. A more detailed label (callsign + speed + rate of descend + etc.) can be used when more information is required.

Controller controls a given space chunk (within a range of altitudes) and the routes passing through it. Controllers have to pay special attention to the points where two or more routes cross. They have to anticipate conflicts early, act early to prevent them, and monitor the effectiveness of the action until the aircrafts are clear of each other. There may be military excluded areas within that space chunk that the flights should not cross. Some flights go at cruising levels, but others are descending to land, or climbing to get their cruising level. Furthermore, the sector has its own rules.

Example of some sector rules:

- Any descending flight must exit the sector at the lowest altitude of the sector (if not possible, the controller has to coordinate with the controller of an adjacent sector).
- Flights have to be 5 nm apart or, at least, be 1000 ft vertically separated.
- If 2 aircrafts follow the same route an go at the same level, they need to be 10 nm apart with the faster one in front when leaving the sector.

The controller can predict whether 2 or more flights will be together in X minutes. Some methods for keeping flights separated when using radar are:

- Make flight A decrease speed and B increase it.
- Make A, B, or both, temporarily alter their trayectory (horizontally, vertically, or both).

## ATM/ANS main actors in Europe

**Global and European**:

- **ICAO** (International Civil Aviation Organization): World authority in aviation matters (including ATM). It produces standards and recommended practices, it armonices and modernizes the air transport industry.
- **EU** (European Union): Main responsible of ATM legislation in Europe. It tries to armonize ATM in Europe with the **SES** (Single European Sky), which tries to meet future capacity demands and safety needs. This legislation targets at safety, network capacity, effectiveness, and environmental impact.
  - **Network manager**: He manages the network functions (airspace design, network management, …).
  - **EASA** (European Aviation Safety Agency): Single european regulator. It issues the regulations that ensure safety in European ATM (requirements for organizations providing air navigation services, ATCs training & licensing, common rules of the air, …). Once a regulation is approved by the EU parlament, the EASA is responsible for ensuring that each member state fulfills his obligations regarding that regulation.
  - **SESAR** (SAS ATM research program): Major public-private cross-industry program that aims at improving ATM performance. Developed by EU, EUROCONTROL, and some ATM industry actors (manufacturers, air navigation service providers, …). It develops new concepts and technologies for modernizing Europe ATM systems. It’s managed by a deployment manager (partnerships of ANSPs, Airlines, and European airports).
  - **FABS** (Functional Airspace Blocks): It considers that the size and shape of an airspace should be determined by the traffic flow and the need of the airspace industry. In Europe, the airspace boundaries are mostly aligned with national boundaries, which is not necessarily the most efficient way of managing air traffic flows. Some European airspaces should be grouped together in FABS, which would provide more efficiency, easier optimization, more capacity, more safety, and less ANS’ costs.
  - **EUROCONTROL** (since 1960): Intergovernmental multi-disciplined organization completely dedicated to ATM, and made up of 41 member states who drive its agenda. It deals with civil and military organizations. It’s committed to build, together with his partners, a SES that will deliver the ATM performance required for the future. It also ensures that the interests of all aviation stakeholders (airspace users, ANS providers, airports, …) are represented. It organizes its activities in 5 main areas:
    - Network manager directorate: It keeps air network running smoothly and efficiently, supports ANS providers and airports, delivers training, and manages scarce resources.
    - Directorate ATM: It leads and coordinates ATM developments, makes sure that research supports SESAR, and provides military-military & civil-military coordination.
    - Directorate Pan-European Single Sky: It delivers Agency’s contribution to the implementation of the SES.
    - Directorate Central Route Charges Office: Collect en-route charges on behalf of 43 states.
    - Maastrich Upper Area Control Centre: It provides air traffic services in the upper airspace of Belgium, Luxembourg, Netherlands, and Germany (north west).

**National level**:

The ATM environment provides air traffic services, supervision of these services, and representation of organization at European and international level. This is the responsibility of local organizations in each country such as:

- **CAA** (Civil Aviation Authority): It oversees the approval and regulation of civil aviation. It issues regulations within the bounds determined by the parlament in a Civil Aviation Act or similar. In Europe, it consults with EASA at regulatory matters. It typically cares about the design of aircrafts or safety equipment, aircraft maintenance and operations, licensing of pilots and ATCs, standards for ATC, regulation for ANSPs, and (sometimes) investigation of aviation accidents. In Europe, it regulates air traffic by applying and supervising EU regulations. It cannot provide ANSs.
- **ANSP** (ANS provider): It’s a public entity that may provide ATM, communications, navigation, surveillance systems, meteorological services, and aeronautical information services.
- **NSA** (National Supervisory Authority): It’s responsible for supervising aviation entities, ANSPs, and training organizations. This is done to make sure that they comply with the regulations.

**Main associations**:

- **CANSO** (Civil Air Navigation Services Organization): It puts together ANSPs worldwide.
- **ACI** (Airports Council International): It represents airports worldwide.
- **IATA** (Internation Air Transport Association): It represents over 250 airlines worldwide.
- **IFATCA** (International Federation of ATCs Associations): It represents the interests and needs of ATCs.
- **IFATSEA** (International Federation of Air Traffic Safety Electronics Associations): It represents the interests and need of technical and engineering staff.
- **IFALPA** (International Federation of Air Line Pilots Associations): It represents airline pilots at large.

## Challenges, objectives, measures

**ATM challenges**: To provide safe and efficient services in an environment with increasing demand for more capacity (for more flights). The complications are:

- European airspace is very busy
- The easier solutions have already been implemented
- The coordination between so many sovereign states is difficult

**EU objectives**:

- To enable a 3-fold increase in capacity (reduces delays in ground and air)
- To improve safety by a factor of 10
- To reduce by 10% the environmental effects from flights
- To provide ATM services to airspace users at 10% less cost

**Measures**: Done by addressing challenges in a concrete way, which includes:

- Management at a European level of the European ATM network (Network manager)
- Trans-state regulation at EU level to harmonize systems, procedures, and routings (EASA).
- Establishment of a European ATM Master Plan and more detailed objectives by stakeholder and by performance area, the setting of a public-private partnerships for research and development (SESAR)
- Obligation of setup of blocks of airspace that consider the functional need of air traffic routes rather than the state political constraints (FABS).

**Network manager (NM)**:

Europe airspace is made of many flight information regions. Within these regions, ATC is provided by the different ANSPs. The European traffic network is dense and complex. During some periods of the day, some routes and airspaces are close to reach their capacity. Since this airspace crosses many national boundaries, a lot of planning and coordination is required to keep maximum efficiency. The NM is a European centralized function that carries out this planning and coordination in collaboration with stakeholders and partners (airspace users, airports, control centers, EU/non-EU states). The EU nominates EUROCONTROL as NM, which is governed by the Network Manager Board and the European Single Sky Committee.

- The NM works with ANSPs to create flexible and dynamic airspace structures to increase the network’s maximum capacity and reduce delays (example: to free non-used military airspace temporarily for civil flights).
- The NM operation center balances capacity and demand (it aims to avoid overloading the air traffic control sectors and limiting and limiting on-flight holding). All FPLs are sent to the NM for processing into the overall system (it can be a month in advance, but it’s usually 3 hours before leaving the gate).
- The NM works with ANSPs and airlines to reduce the weather impact by proposing alternative routes, managing traffic flows, and sharing impact assessments with airlines, airports, and ANSPs. The NM monitors and helps to prevent the impacts of other events (industrial actions, vulcanic eruptions, space weather, armed conflicts, security incidents, …). Optional measures can be deployed by the NM at short notice to respond to unusual disruptions. If this is a major crisis or causes prolonged disruption in air traffic network, the European Aviation Crisis Coordination Cell can be activated to bring together the experts and stakeholders to coordinate the management of the response to the crisis.
- The NM ensures that Europe’s network is constantly evolving to meet increasing traffic demands.
- The NM is constantly developing new tools and ways to increase capacity, working with partners to improve the air traffic flow and capacity management processes, sharing information, and collaboratively making decisions.

**Addressing challenges**:

- **Cost & efficiency**:

  - **Harmonization** of facilities, resources, and common procurement processes can reduce the expenses of the provision of services. This is done, in part, by the EU within the SES regulatory framework which requires member states to group together into FABs.
  - **Interoperability** of ATC systems, as well as interfaces between systems, is a technological solution to reducing costs and improve efficiency.
  - **Research & development**: Partnerships and alliances have been formed to carry out R&D through the SESAR programme and to deploy the resulting tools.
  
- **Efficiency & safety**: Sharing information well helps make decisions at the right time with the needed information. Technology helps share information dynamically, and various protocols enable us to make decision collaboratively and individually.

  - **SWIM** (System Wide Information Management): It’s the ATM intranet. It enables seamless information interchange between all providers and users of ATM information. This is aeronautical, airport, flight, meteorology, surveillance, flow, and capacity & demand information. SWIM gives the right person the right information at the right time in a secure way. Stakeholders using SWIM gain from improved situation awareness, efficient stakeholders collaboration, and enhances decision making. The interactions between ATM stakeholders is standardized to make them interoperable. Combining three standards (Service, Information, Technical) makes it possible to achieve organizational, semantic and technical interoperability in all ATM stakeholder’s interactions. The standards are agree through the SWIM governance process stored in the SWIM registry. This registry is open for use by all ATM stakeholders (airspace users, ANSPs, airports, network manager, …). Manufactures can develop SWIM applications and services. SESAR projects have developed prototypes on the SWIM infrastructure and SWIM enabled applications. The SWIM initiative in SESAR, the global ATM is becoming more interoperable, efficient, and cost-effective. The 3 standards are:
    - Service standards: It describes how the actors can achieve organization and interoperability through coordinated processes and behaviors. Described in the ISRM (Information Services Reference Model).
    - Information standards: Describe information in a way that it’s understood in the same way by all actors. Described in the AIRM (ATM Information Reference Model).
    - Technical standards: Describe how the supporting technical systems interact and how data is transported. Described in the SWIM-TI (SWIM-Technical Infrastructure Profiles).
  - **A-CDM** (Airport Collaborative Decision Making): Developed by EUROCONTROL. This encourages information sharing and collaborative decision making to increase information efficiency and performance, and make the best use of infrastructure. This ensures that airport operators receive accurate aircraft information, and aircraft operators receive accurate operational data, and the CFMU (Central Flow Management Unit) receives accurate information about future flights. It aims to enhance information sharing, encourage collaborative decision making, and prompt reaction times in order to make the best use of everyone times and resources. Main partners in A-CDM: aircraft operators, air traffic service providers, airport operators, CFMU, ground handlers, and service providers.
